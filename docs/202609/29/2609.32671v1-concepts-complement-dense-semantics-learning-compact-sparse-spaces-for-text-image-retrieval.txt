## ABSTRACT 

Cross-modal retrieval has been advanced by vision-language pre-trained models that encode images and texts into a shared dense embedding space. While dense representations effectively capture overall semantic similarity, they often obscure fine-grained visual-textual information needed for precise cross-modal matching. Re-cent methods introduce a learned sparse branch to complement dense matching with lexical evidence, but they rely on a redundant language-model token space and lack explicit grounding for sparse dimensions. We propose GRASP, a compact and grounded sparse learning framework that mines visual-textual concepts from the corpus. A lightweight sparse head is trained to predict concepts relevant to each image or text, yielding interpretable concept-level evidence that complements dense semantic matching. Extensive experiments show that GRASP improves retrieval accuracy over the state-of-the-art dense-sparse baselines while yielding a more compact and grounded sparse space.

However, dense representa-tions have been reported to underrepresent fine-grained signals, limiting their effectiveness for queries that require precise matching of detailed content, such as specific entities or attributes [9, 12, 23]. To supply fine-grained signals, prior studies have incorporated explicit knowledge into dense retrievers. Such knowledge is often derived from domain-specific structures, such as product attributes (e.g., brand, category) in e-commerce search [1, 12, 23] or academic topics (e.g., fields of study) in scientific search [9, 13], and com-bined with dense embeddings to form enriched representations for granular matching. However, these approaches have largely been limited to single-modal, domain-specific settings. In cross-modal retrieval, a closely related line of work integrates learned sparse retrieval with dense models [3, 18, 22], where images and texts are additionally projected into the token space of a language model. By representing inputs over lexical dimensions, the sparse branch cap-tures token-level fine-grained information, offering complementary guidance to dense models during both training and retrieval. 1

The state-of-the-art method, Sparse and Dense Learn Better To-gether (SDLBT) [22], encourages dense and sparse branches to learn from each other through bidirectional distillation. It computes a 

teacher score by combining cross-modal similarities from the dense and sparse branches, and uses this score to supervise both branches. This joint optimization benefits both branches, particularly improv-ing the standalone retrieval accuracy of the sparse branch. Still, this design has two limitations. First, SDLBT relies on the full token space of a language model, which is overly redundant for image-text retrieval. Many dimensions are weakly related to visual content, including discourse tokens (e.g., “therefore”, “however”) and fragmented subword units (e.g., “##ny”, “##ism”). This bloated space increases computational overhead, obscures meaningful lexi-cal evidence, and degrades interpretability. We argue that the sparse space should be compact and tailored to retrieval-relevant concepts. Second, SDLBT lacks an explicit mechanism to preserve explicit grounding of the sparse branch, which can cause activated tokens to drift away from meaningful visual-textual concepts. The sparse projection is largely trained to mimic the teacher score, without 

direct supervision that encourages meaningful lexical tokens to be activated for each image or text. As a result, the sparse branch

## 2 PRELIMINARIES 2.1 Problem Formulation 

We consider a cross-modal dataset D consisting of images and their associated texts (e.g., descriptions). For each image 𝑖 , T𝑖 denotes its associated text set. Following [22], we focus on text-to-image retrieval, where a textual query is used to retrieve images in D.This setting reflects real-world visual content retrieval applications. 

## 2.2 Dense Retrieval with Learned Sparse Head 

We describe SDLBT [22], a dense-sparse retrieval framework with a sparse head on top of the backbone VLP, which forms the basis of this work. Let 𝑓 denote a VLP model that encodes text and im-age inputs into contextual embeddings. Given a text 𝑡 (or image 

𝑖 ), 𝑓 encodes lexical token (or visual patch) sequences into contex-tual embeddings. These embeddings are pooled (e.g., CLS or mean pooling) to obtain dense representations h𝑡 , h𝑖 ∈ R𝑑 .

Dense retrieval. Dense retrieval computes cross-modal similarity as 𝑠 dense (𝑡, 𝑖 ) = sim (h𝑡 , h𝑖 ). As each input is compressed into a sin-gle dense vector, it mainly captures the overall semantic similarity. 

Learned sparse branch. To supply fine-grained lexical signals, a sparse projection head is added on top of the backbone VLP model. Specifically, a projection head 𝑔 : R𝑑 → R| V | maps dense repre-sentations into a vocabulary-sized token space V.2 A sparsifying activation function [7] is then applied to obtain non-negative sparse weights: z∗ = log (1 + ReLU (𝑔 (h∗))) . The sparse similarity is com-puted as 𝑠 sparse (𝑡, 𝑖 ) = sim (z𝑡 , z𝑖 ). Each dimension of z∗ corresponds to a lexical token, allowing the sparse branch to encode token-level matching signals that may be underrepresented in dense vectors.

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

We follow the dense retrieval with a learned sparse head paradigm. Our key distinctions lie in two aspects: how we define the sparse space and how we ensure concept grounding of sparse space (Fig. 1). 

## 3.1 Compact Concept Space Construction 

We construct a compact concept space tailored to text-image re-trieval. The space should bridge the two modalities with fine-grained and interpretable information. Inspired by scene graphs [11, 25], which represent a visual scene through entities and their relations, we use entity- and relation-level concepts as language-based units for connecting visual content with textual descriptions.

## 3.2 Concept-Grounded Relevance Learning 

Given C, we replace the vast token space of the sparse branch with the concept space, with a projection head 𝑔 C : R𝑑 → R| C | .

Sparse concept branch. Unlike SDLBT, which projects the pooled representation h∗, GRASP applies the projection before pooling to better preserve fine-grained signals. Let h∗,𝑚 denote the contextual embedding at position 𝑚 , where positions correspond to tokens or patches. The sparse concept representation is obtained with the sparsifying activation and max pooling over positions:

Concept grounding objective. To ensure that each dimension of 

z∗ accurately reflects its corresponding concept, we train the sparse branch to predict concepts relevant to each input. Using the concept-indexed information, we cast this as a multi-label classification task formulated with binary cross-entropy (BCE): 

Lconcept −ground = LBCE (z𝑖 , c𝑖 ) + L BCE (z𝑡 , c𝑡 ). (6) This concept grounding loss is applied to both modalities, using each image 𝑖 and each associated text 𝑡 ∈ T 𝑖 .This concept prediction learning provides several benefits. First, the sparse branch can infer relevant concepts for unseen images or texts and further supports implicit concept expansion by activating related concepts beyond exact surface matches. Moreover, multi-label supervision penalizes irrelevant concept dimensions, leading to sparse and interpretable concept-grounded representations. 

Integration and optimization. The final retrieval score is ob-tained by combining the dense and sparse similarities. For simplic-ity, we define 𝑠 comb (𝑡, 𝑖 ) = 𝑠 dense (𝑡, 𝑖 ) + 𝑤 · 𝑠 sparse (𝑡, 𝑖 ), where we set 𝑤 = 0.1. The training process follows the base pipeline in §2.2, except that the sparse branch is guided by the concept grounding loss instead of the contrastive loss: 

LGRASP = Lbase + 𝜆 · L concept −ground , (7) where 𝜆 balances the concept grounding loss. We set 𝜆 = 0.5. 

Remarks on efficiency. Concept space construction introduces additional computation, but it is performed only during offline in-dexing phase. During online retrieval, GRASP adds a lightweight projection head, requiring one matrix multiplication over the stacked contextual embeddings followed by max pooling. This introduces negligible overhead compared with the backbone encoder. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We follow the setup of the state-of-the-art method, SDLBT, in-cluding datasets, backbones, and metrics. As the original image descriptions are often short and limited [5, 6], we augment T𝑖 with one LLM-generated description per image. All baselines use the same augmented training data, which is not used for testing. 

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate on two widely used datasets: Flickr30k [21] with 29,000/1,014/1,000 (train / val / test) images and MSCOCO [16] with 113,287/5,000/5,000 images, following the Karpathy split [10]. The concept vocabulary has |𝐶 | = 4,046 on Flickr30k (#ent = 2,964, #rel = 1,082) and |𝐶 | = 9,771 on MSCOCO (#ent = 5,951, #rel = 3,820). We report Recall@ {1, 5} and MRR@10. 

Backbone. We adopt two VLP backbones, ALBEF [15] and BLIP [14], using public checkpoints fine-tuned on each dataset, following [22]. 

Compared methods. We compare GRASP with various recent cross-modal dense-sparse retrieval methods. (1) Dense denotes a standard dense retriever fully fine-tuned with the augmented data. (2) STAIR [3] maps images and text into a shared sparse token space by grounding visual representations to BERT vocabulary tokens. (3) D2S [18] maps fixed dense vectors into a sparse token space with probabilistic term expansion. (4) SDLBT [22] is the state-of-the-art method that leverages the combined dense-sparse score as a stronger teacher signal (§2.2). We report sparse-only (S), dense-only (D), and combined dense-sparse (D+S) results when applicable. As GRASP’s sparse branch is designed to complement dense retrieval, D+S is our main setting. For STAIR, whose original model was trained from scratch with a CLIP-style dual encoder, we reimplement its sparse learning method using the same frozen VLP backbones. ## 4.1 Results 

4.1.1 Overall Evaluation . Table 1 reports retrieval performance averaged over three independent runs. Overall, GRASP consistently achieves the best overall retrieval performance across backbones and datasets, using a substantially smaller concept space than the sparse token-space baselines (i.e., STAIR, D2S, SDLBT). As noted in prior work [5, 6], relevance labels in these benchmarks are often incomplete, leading to false negatives where relevant images are not labeled as positives. For more comprehensive evaluation, we further conduct an LLM-as-a-judge evaluation [4]. GRASP consistently achieves a higher winning ratio, outperforming the best baseline by 10.9% to 27.5%. The improvements of GRASP come from the complementarity of its sparse branch, not from making it a stronger standalone retriever. Indeed, its sparse-only performance is relatively low, as expected from the highly compact sparse space and absence of a contrastive loss. Nevertheless, fusion with the dense branch achieves the best performance in every setting. By contrast, prior sparse branches can show relatively strong sparse-only performance, but they often fail to provide complementary gains after fusion. 4.1.2 In-depth Comparison with SDLBT . We compare GRASP and SDLBT in sparse space design and grounding in Figure 2. 

Sparse space design. We conduct a size-matched comparison by pruning SDLBT’s BERT token space to the top-activated 4K tokens ,matching the dimensionality of our concept space. As shown in Figure 2(a), this simple pruning substantially degrades SDLBT, while GRASP outperforms both the full 30K SDLBT and the 4K pruned variant. This validates the effectiveness of our sparse space design. 

Sparse activation grounding. We analyze the top activated sparse dimensions for an image to examine whether sparse activations are meaningfully grounded. As discussed earlier, SDLBT lacks di-rect grounding supervision. Indeed, SDLBT activates fragmented or weakly grounded tokens (e.g., ##tu , ##lan ). In contrast, GRASP activates interpretable object and relation concepts relevant to the image (e.g., jersey , leaning ), showing that concept grounding pro-vides compact and interpretable evidence.

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We present GRASP, a text-image retrieval framework that addresses two limitations of prior dense-sparse retrieval: a redundant token space and insufficient grounding of sparse dimensions. GRASP in-troduces concept space construction to replace the large token vocab-ulary, and concept-grounded relevance learning to explicitly anchor sparse dimensions to their target concepts. Across Flickr30k and MS-COCO with ALBEF and BLIP backbones, GRASP consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art method while using a substantially smaller sparse space. In future work, we plan to extend concept-grounded sparse learning to more diverse retrieval scenarios.
